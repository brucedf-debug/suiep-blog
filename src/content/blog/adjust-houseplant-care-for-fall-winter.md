Many houseplant problems that show up in December start in September and October, when the plant's environment changes but the care routine doesn't. Shorter days, weaker light and drier heated air all change what a plant needs. A watering rhythm that was right in July can drown the same plant by November. The good news is that most of the adjustment is doing less, not more: you're matching your routine to a plant that is slowing down.

**Quick answer:** As days shorten, water only when the soil is dry at the depth your plant prefers, which usually means less often than in summer. Move plants closer to the brightest windows, clean the glass and leaves, and add a grow light on a timer for plants that stretch or drop leaves. Stop fertilizing most plants from late fall until new growth appears in spring. Raise humidity for tropical plants, keep them away from heaters and cold drafts, and hold off on repotting until spring.

## What Changes in Fall and Winter

| Factor | Summer | Fall and winter | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daylight | Long, strong | Short, weak, lower sun angle | Move plants closer to windows; consider grow lights |
| Water use | High | Much lower | Check soil before watering; water less often |
| Growth | Active | Slow or paused | Stop or reduce fertilizer |
| Humidity | Often moderate | Often low with heating | Humidifier, grouping, keep away from vents |
| Temperature | Stable | Drafts near windows and doors | Keep tropicals away from cold glass at night |

## Water Less, and Water by the Soil

With less light, plants photosynthesize less and use water more slowly, so the soil stays wet longer. Watering on the summer schedule is the most common way houseplants get overwatered in winter.

- **Check first, every time.** Push a finger into the soil or lift the pot; dry soil is noticeably lighter.
- **Match the plant.** Succulents and snake plants should dry out almost completely. Most tropical foliage plants can be watered when the top 1–2 inches are dry. Ferns and calatheas like more even moisture, but still not soggy soil.
- **Water thoroughly, then wait.** Small sips keep the surface wet while roots stay dry. Water until it drains, then let the soil dry to the right depth again.
- **Use room-temperature water.** Cold water can shock tropical roots.

For many plants, this naturally stretches the interval to every 10–14 days or longer, but the soil check matters more than any number.

## Chase the Light as It Moves

The sun sits lower through fall and winter, and a spot that was bright all summer can become dim by November.

- **Walk the house at midday** and notice where the light falls now. South-facing windows generally give the most winter light in the Northern Hemisphere.
- **Move plants closer.** A plant that was fine several feet from a window in summer may need to be right beside it.
- **Clean windows and leaves.** Dust on both reduces light more than people think.
- **Rotate a quarter turn** every week or two for even growth.

Leggy stems, smaller new leaves and leaning toward the glass are signs of too little light. If a plant was borderline in summer, fall is the time to move it or add a light.

## When to Use a Grow Light

A full-spectrum LED grow light is an easy fix for dim rooms. A small clip-on or panel light a foot or so above the foliage, on a timer for 10–12 hours a day, can offset weak winter light. The timer matters: consistency is what plants respond to. Not every plant needs one; a healthy plant growing a bit slower in winter is normal. Use a light for plants that are dropping leaves, stretching or losing variegation.

## Pause Fertilizer

Most houseplants slow down in winter. Fertilizing a plant that isn't growing lets salts build up in the soil, which can burn roots. For most houseplants, stop feeding from late fall and resume when you see new growth in spring. Plants still actively growing under strong grow lights or in bright, warm rooms can get a light feed at a quarter to half strength once a month. Our [fertilizing guide](/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants/) covers the details.

## Protect Against Dry Air, Heat and Drafts

Heating dries indoor air, often below what tropical plants prefer. Crispy brown tips and edges in winter usually point to low humidity, not thirst, so don't respond by watering more.

- Run a humidifier near grouped tropical plants. See our guide to [raising humidity in winter](/blog/raise-humidity-for-houseplants-in-winter/).
- Keep plants out of the path of heating vents, radiators and space heaters.
- At night, cold air near windows can chill leaves that touch the glass. Pull plants back a little or don't trap them behind closed curtains.
- Keep plants away from exterior doors that open often.

## Pests Love Winter Too

Warm, dry indoor air favors spider mites, and slower plant growth can make scale and mealybugs more noticeable. Check leaf undersides every couple of weeks, wipe leaves with a damp cloth, and isolate any plant with pests. Overwatered winter soil also invites [fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/).

## Hold Off on Repotting

Repotting is best in spring, when plants recover quickly. In winter, only repot in an emergency like root rot or a broken pot. See our [repotting guide](/blog/how-to-repot-a-houseplant/) for how to do it gently.

## Seasonal Checklist

| When | Tasks |
|---|---|
| Early fall | Bring outdoor plants inside ([how and when](/blog/when-to-bring-houseplants-indoors/)); check for pests; start checking soil before every watering |
| Mid fall | Move plants closer to bright windows; set up grow lights; clean leaves and glass; stop fertilizing most plants |
| Heating season starts | Add a humidifier; move plants away from vents; watch for crispy edges and spider mites |
| Midwinter | Keep watering by soil check; rotate plants; don't repot unless necessary |
| Late winter | Watch for new growth; plan spring repotting; resume feeding when growth restarts |

## Signs Your Plant Hasn't Adjusted

| Sign | Likely cause | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow lower leaves, soil damp for over a week | Overwatering for winter's slower pace | Water less often; improve drainage |
| Leggy stems, smaller leaves, leaning | Too little light | Move closer to a window; add a grow light |
| Crispy brown edges, soil moist | Dry air | Raise humidity; move away from heat |
| Sudden leaf drop | Cold draft or temperature swing | Move away from doors and cold glass |
| Fine webbing, speckled leaves | Spider mites | Rinse leaves; treat; raise humidity |

Because growth is slow in winter, any fix takes a few weeks to show. Give a change time before trying something else.

## FAQ

### How often should I water houseplants in winter?

There's no fixed schedule. Check the soil and water when it's dry to the right depth for that plant. For many plants, that ends up less often than in summer.

### Should I stop fertilizing houseplants in winter?

For most plants, yes, from late fall until new growth appears in spring. Plants growing actively under grow lights can get diluted monthly feeding.

### Is it normal for houseplants to drop leaves in fall?

A few older leaves dropping as light decreases is normal, especially after a move indoors. Many leaves dropping quickly suggests a draft, overwatering or not enough light.

### Do I need a grow light in winter?

Not always. Plants near bright windows usually manage. Grow lights help in dim rooms or for plants that stretch, lose color or drop leaves.
