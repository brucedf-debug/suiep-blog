"Low light" doesn't mean "no light." Every plant needs some light to make food, even if it's just the ambient glow from a north-facing window across the room. What the plants below have in common is that they tolerate dim conditions far better than most houseplants, grow slowly enough that a shortage of light doesn't starve them, and forgive the watering mistakes that low light tends to cause.

**Quick answer:** For the darkest spots in a home, start with a snake plant, ZZ plant or cast iron plant. For a shelf or hanging basket, pick pothos or heartleaf philodendron. If you have cats or dogs, the safe choices on this list are the cast iron plant, parlor palm and spider plant. In any low-light spot, water less often than you think you should. Overwatering kills far more low-light plants than darkness does.

## How to Tell How Much Light a Spot Really Gets

Before choosing a plant, check the spot you have in mind. People's eyes adjust to dim rooms so well that most of us badly overestimate indoor light.

- **The shadow test.** Around midday, hold your hand about a foot above the spot where the plant will sit. A crisp, sharp-edged shadow means bright light. A soft but visible shadow means medium light. A faint, blurry shadow or none at all means low light.
- **Distance from the window.** Light drops off quickly as you move away from glass. A spot a few feet from a window can get several times less light than the sill itself, and a corner on the opposite wall gets less again.
- **Window direction.** North-facing windows (in the Northern Hemisphere) give gentle, even light all day. East windows get soft morning sun. South and west windows are brighter but can be shaded by trees, buildings or deep eaves.
- **A light meter app.** Free phone apps give a rough reading. They aren't lab-accurate, but they are good for comparing two spots in your home.

If your "low light" spot fails the shadow test entirely and is far from any window, treat it as a no-light spot. See the grow-light section below.

## 1. Snake Plant (Dracaena trifasciata)

This one is nearly indestructible. It tolerates low light and irregular watering, and its upright leaves fit tight spaces like hallways. It grows slowly, so it won't quickly outgrow a small pot. It also handles the [dry air](/blog/raise-humidity-for-houseplants-in-winter/) of centrally heated apartments better than most houseplants. In a dim spot, watering every few weeks is often plenty.

![A healthy snake plant in a ceramic pot on a windowsill](../../assets/blog/low-light-plants-snake-plant.jpg)

## 2. ZZ Plant (Zamioculcas zamiifolia)

It stores water in its thick rhizomes, so it thrives on neglect. Its glossy leaves reflect what little light is available. A ZZ plant is more likely to suffer from too much care than too little. Overwatering is the main way people accidentally kill one, and in low light the soil stays wet even longer.

## 3. Pothos (Epipremnum aureum)

A trailing vine that adapts to almost any light level. Variegated varieties need somewhat more light to keep their pattern, but a plain green pothos will happily trail from a shelf with little attention. It roots easily in water, so one healthy plant can become several.

## 4. Cast Iron Plant (Aspidistra elatior)

It earned its name as a Victorian parlor plant that survived gas lighting and cold rooms. It's slow-growing but extremely tolerant of low light and inconsistent care, a good match for an office desk or a stairwell with almost no natural light.

## 5. Peace Lily (Spathiphyllum)

It droops dramatically when thirsty, then perks back up within hours of watering. That's useful feedback for beginners who aren't yet confident reading a plant. It can bloom in fairly dim conditions, though it flowers more reliably with medium light.

## 6. Chinese Evergreen (Aglaonema)

It comes in many leaf patterns, from solid deep green to silvery variegation. As a rule of thumb, the darker green types handle lower light than the pink- or red-toned ones. It grows slowly, so [repotting](/blog/how-to-repot-a-houseplant/) is rarely urgent, and it forgives the occasional missed watering.

## 7. Philodendron (Heartleaf Variety)

As tough as pothos, with softer, heart-shaped leaves and a slightly faster trailing habit. When it needs water, its leaves soften slightly well before it looks genuinely distressed.

## 8. Parlor Palm (Chamaedorea elegans)

A compact palm that actually prefers indirect or low light to direct sun, which makes it unusual among palms. It stays a manageable size for years and adds a feathery texture that the broad-leafed plants on this list don't.

## 9. Dracaena (Corn Plant or Dragon Tree Varieties)

Upright and architectural, and tolerant of low light once established, though it prefers to dry out well between waterings. Dracaenas are sensitive to fluoride and salts in tap water, which can show up as browning leaf tips. Switching to filtered or rain water usually helps.

## 10. Spider Plant (Chlorophytum comosum)

Spider plants tolerate low-to-medium light rather than truly dark corners, so give them the brightest of your dim spots. They are famously easy to [propagate](/blog/propagate-houseplants-from-cuttings/): the small plantlets that dangle from mature plants root readily in water. They're also non-toxic to cats and dogs.

## How to Care for Plants in Low Light

Low light changes almost every part of plant care, because the plant grows and drinks more slowly.

- **Water by checking the soil, not the calendar.** Push a finger into the soil. For most plants on this list, water only when the top inch or two is dry; for snake plants and ZZ plants, wait until the pot is nearly dry throughout. Soil that dries in a week by a bright window can stay damp for two or three weeks in a dim corner.
- **Always use pots with drainage holes.** Slow water uptake plus no drainage is the most common recipe for root rot in low light.
- **Wipe dust off the leaves every month or so.** Dust builds up gradually and acts like shade cloth over the leaves.
- **Rotate pots a quarter turn every couple of weeks** so growth stays even when light comes from one side.
- **Go easy on fertilizer.** Slow-growing plants use little of it. A diluted feed a few times during spring and summer is usually enough, and none in winter.

## When You Need a Grow Light

If a spot is truly windowless, or the shadow test shows nothing at all, even the toughest plant here will slowly decline. A basic full-spectrum LED grow light on a timer solves this cheaply. Put it on a simple outlet timer so the plant gets a consistent day length, and keep the light close enough that it actually reaches the leaves. Most small grow lights lose effectiveness quickly with distance. Regular overhead room lighting helps a little, but it's rarely enough on its own.

## Troubleshooting Low-Light Problems

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Long, stretched stems with big gaps between leaves | Not enough light | Move closer to a window or add a grow light; prune leggy stems to encourage fuller growth |
| Variegated leaves turning plain green | Not enough light | Move to a brighter spot; new leaves should show pattern again |
| Lower leaves yellowing, soil still damp | Overwatering | Let soil dry further between waterings; check roots for rot |
| Mushy stem base or sour smell from soil | Root rot | Remove the plant, cut away brown, soft roots, repot in fresh, dry mix |
| Brown, crispy leaf tips | Dry air, salt or fluoride buildup | Raise humidity, flush the soil with plenty of water, switch water source |
| No new growth for months in winter | Normal seasonal slowdown | Reduce watering; growth resumes in spring |

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Watering on a fixed schedule.** Weekly watering that works for a sunny windowsill drowns a plant in a dim corner.
- **Moving a plant straight from a dark corner into strong sun.** Leaves adapted to low light can scorch. Increase light gradually over a week or two.
- **Assuming all "low-light" varieties are equal.** Brightly variegated or colorful cultivars of the same species usually need more light than the plain green form.
- **Repotting into a much bigger pot.** Extra soil holds extra water that a slow-growing plant can't use, which invites rot. Go up only one pot size at a time.

## FAQ

### Can any of these plants live in a windowless bathroom?

Not long-term on light alone. A snake plant or ZZ plant may hang on for months, but it will slowly decline. Adding a small grow light on a timer, or rotating the plant to a window every couple of weeks, makes a windowless room workable.

### Does office fluorescent or LED lighting count?

It helps, especially if the lights stay on for most of the day and the plant sits close to them. Many ZZ plants and pothos do fine on office desks under ceiling lights. The further the plant is from the fixture, the less it gets.

### How often should I water a plant in low light?

There's no fixed interval; it depends on the pot, soil, temperature and plant. Check the soil with your finger and water thoroughly only when the right depth has dried out. Expect much longer gaps in winter than in summer.

### Why is my low-light plant not growing at all?

Slow growth is normal for these species, especially from late fall to early spring. If there is no growth at all during the warm months, the spot is likely too dark. Try moving the plant closer to a window or add a grow light.
