Fall produces more compost material than any other season: leaves, spent garden plants and more kitchen scraps as cooking moves indoors. A loose pile in the corner of the yard will break down eventually, but it sprawls, invites pests and looks messy the whole time. A bin made from wood pallets fixes all three problems for very little money, since pallets are often free and already have most of the structure built in.

**Quick answer:** Use four heat-treated (HT) pallets in sound condition. Set a stake or post at each corner on bare soil, screw three pallets to the posts to form a U, and hinge the fourth as a gate with a latch. Line the inside with hardware cloth if rodents are a concern. Start with coarse material at the bottom, then layer about two to three parts browns to one part greens, keep it moist and turn it every week or two.

## Project Overview

| Detail | Estimate |
|---|---|
| **Size** | About 4 × 4 ft inside, 3–4 ft tall (with standard pallets) |
| **Capacity** | Roughly a cubic yard or more of material |
| **Difficulty** | Beginner |
| **Build time** | 2–4 hours |
| **Main costs** | Pallets are often free; posts, screws, hinges and hardware cloth are the main expenses |

## What You'll Need

**Materials**
- 4 wood pallets of similar size (5–7 for a two-bay system)
- 4 wooden stakes or short 4x4 posts, about 4–5 ft long
- 2½–3 in exterior-rated screws
- 2–3 heavy-duty exterior hinges and a hook-and-eye latch or barrel bolt
- Optional: ½ in hardware cloth and heavy staples, zip ties for a quick build

**Tools**
- Drill/driver
- Sledgehammer or post driver
- Level, tape measure
- Work gloves and eye protection

## Step 1: Choose Safe Pallets

| Stamp or condition | Meaning | Use for compost? |
|---|---|---|
| **HT** | Heat-treated | Yes |
| **KD** | Kiln-dried | Generally fine |
| **MB** | Methyl bromide fumigated | No |
| No stamp | Unknown history | Only if you know the source |
| Colored (blue, red) pallets | Usually rental pallets | No, they belong to a pallet company |
| Chemical stains or smells | Past spills | No |

Always ask before taking pallets from a business. Choose ones with solid slats and blocks, without rot or large splits.

## Step 2: Pick the Site

- **Bare soil,** not concrete or a deck, so water drains and worms can move in.
- **Partial sun:** enough warmth to keep the pile active without drying it out.
- **Convenient but not too close:** near the kitchen door or garden, but not right beside the patio or a neighbor's fence.
- **Level ground** makes the build much easier.
- **Room to work:** leave space in front for opening the gate and a wheelbarrow.

## Step 3: Set the Corner Posts

Lay out the pallets on the ground in a square to mark the corners. Standard North American pallets are about 48 × 40 inches, so the bin won't be perfectly square unless your pallets match; that's fine. Drive a stake at each corner, about 12–18 inches into the ground, and check that each is plumb. In loose or sandy soil, go deeper. The stakes carry the pallets, so they need to be solid.

## Step 4: Build Three Walls

Stand a pallet against the back pair of posts and screw through its frame into each post at the top and bottom. Repeat for the two side walls, so the three pallets form a U. Where two pallets meet at a corner, screw or zip-tie them together as well as to the post. Check the walls are plumb as you go.

## Step 5: Hinge the Front Gate

Attach the fourth pallet to one front post with two or three heavy-duty hinges so it swings open like a gate. Add a latch on the other side to keep it closed against wind and animals. The gate makes it easy to add material, turn the pile and shovel finished compost out from the bottom.

A simpler alternative: slide the front panel between pairs of stakes so it lifts out, or use removable horizontal boards that you add as the pile grows.

## Step 6: Line It (Optional but Worthwhile)

Staple ½-inch hardware cloth to the inside of the three fixed walls, and across the bottom under the pile if rats or voles are common in your area. It keeps small scraps from falling out and keeps rodents from getting in, while still letting air through.

## Step 7: Load the Bin

1. Start with a few inches of coarse material, such as twigs or straw, for airflow at the bottom.
2. Add layers of **browns** (dry leaves, cardboard, straw) and **greens** (kitchen scraps, grass clippings, coffee grounds), aiming for about two to three parts browns to one part greens by volume.
3. Bury food scraps in the middle under a layer of browns to avoid smells and flies.
4. Moisten the pile so it feels like a wrung-out sponge.

Fall is ideal: [raked leaves](/blog/how-to-rake-leaves-efficiently/) give you a big supply of browns. Store a few bags to mix in through winter. Leave out meat, dairy, oily food and pet waste. Our full guide to [composting at home](/blog/how-to-compost-at-home/) covers what goes in and what stays out.

## Step 8: Turn and Maintain

Every week or two, open the gate and turn the pile with a garden fork, moving outside material into the center where it's warmest. Water in dry spells, and add browns if it gets soggy.

## Expand to a Two- or Three-Bay System

Once one bin fills, add a second bay by attaching two more pallets that share a wall with the first. Fill one side while the other finishes, then turn material from the active bay into the next. A three-bay system (new, active, finished) keeps compost coming steadily.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Smells like ammonia or rotten eggs | Too many greens or too wet | Mix in dry browns; turn to add air |
| Nothing breaks down | Too dry, too many browns, or too small a pile | Water while turning; add greens; fill the bin fuller |
| Rodents | Food scraps exposed, gaps in walls or floor | Bury scraps; line with hardware cloth; skip meat and dairy |
| Walls leaning | Stakes too shallow | Drive stakes deeper or add a second stake per corner |
| Gate sagging | Light hinges or rotten frame | Use heavy hinges; add a block under the free end |
| Pallets rotting | Normal over years of damp contact | Replace individual pallets as needed |

## Using Finished Compost

Finished compost is dark, crumbly and smells earthy. Shovel it from the bottom through the gate, screen out large pieces and return them to the pile. Spread it on beds in spring, mix it into [raised beds](/blog/how-to-build-a-raised-garden-bed/), or top-dress around perennials.

## FAQ

### Are pallets safe for a compost bin?

Heat-treated (HT) pallets are generally considered fine. Avoid MB-stamped pallets, pallets with chemical stains or smells, and rental pallets.

### How big should a compost bin be?

About 3–4 feet on each side holds enough material to heat up well. Smaller piles break down more slowly.

### Do I need a bottom on the bin?

No. Bare soil lets water drain and worms move in. Add hardware cloth underneath only if burrowing rodents are a problem.

### How long until I get compost?

With a well-managed pile, a few months. A casual pile can take closer to a year.
