A raised garden bed is one of the fastest ways to go from bare lawn to a productive garden. The soil warms earlier in spring, drains better than most yard soil, and stays loose because nobody walks on it. The frame is also a clear boundary that keeps grass out and makes the garden easier to manage. This plan builds a 4 × 8 foot bed about 18 inches deep, a size that fits most yards and lets you reach the middle from either side.

**Quick answer:** Choose a level spot with at least 6 hours of direct sun. Build a frame from untreated cedar boards screwed to 4x4 corner posts, two boards high. Lay cardboard over the grass, fill with a mix of roughly 60% quality topsoil, 30% compost and 10% perlite or other drainage material, water it in, and plant. Expect a few hours for the frame plus the time to move about 1.8 cubic yards of soil.

## Choosing the Material

| Material | Lifespan | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cedar or redwood | Many years | Naturally rot-resistant; the classic choice for edibles |
| Pine or fir (untreated) | A few seasons | Cheap but rots quickly in contact with soil |
| Modern pressure-treated lumber | Long | Current treatments are considered much safer than old arsenic-based ones, but many gardeners still avoid it for food beds; if used, line the inside |
| Galvanized steel kits | Long | Quick to assemble; can get hot in full sun |
| Stone or block | Very long | Heavier work, most permanent |

This guide uses cedar, which balances cost, lifespan and ease of building.

## What You'll Need

**Materials**
- 4 cedar boards, 2 × 10 in × 8 ft (long sides, two per side)
- 2 cedar boards, 2 × 10 in × 8 ft, cut in half for the short sides (four 4-ft pieces)
- 1 cedar 4 × 4 post, cut into four 18-in corner posts
- 3-in exterior deck screws
- Cardboard (plain, tape removed) or landscape fabric for the base
- Optional: hardware cloth if gophers or voles are a problem

**Tools**
- Drill/driver and bits
- Circular saw (or have the lumber yard make the cuts)
- Tape measure, speed square, level
- Shovel, rake and wheelbarrow

## Step 1: Choose and Prep the Site

Pick a spot with at least 6 hours of direct sun, preferably more for tomatoes and peppers. Watch it through a full day if you're unsure, since shade from buildings and trees shifts more than you'd expect. Put it within easy hose reach; a bed that's a chore to water gets watered less.

Mark the footprint, remove large rocks and roughly level the ground. You don't need to dig out the grass. Cardboard laid inside the frame will smother it.

## Step 2: Cut the Boards

- Keep four 8-ft boards whole for the long sides.
- Cut the remaining two 8-ft boards in half for four 4-ft short sides.
- Cut the 4 × 4 into four 18-in corner posts.

Most lumber yards will make straight cuts for free or for a small fee, which saves time even if you own a saw.

## Step 3: Assemble the Frame

1. Stand two corner posts on a flat surface and screw a short board to them, flush with the top of the posts. Use two or three screws per board end and pre-drill to avoid splitting the cedar.
2. Add the second short board beneath the first to make a side about 18½ in tall (a 2 × 10 actually measures about 9¼ in wide).
3. Repeat for the other short side.
4. Connect the two short sides with the long boards the same way.
5. Check the frame for square by measuring both diagonals. They should match. Adjust before you fill it; a frame full of soil won't move.

For long beds, a cross brace or a center stake on each long side keeps the boards from bowing outward under the weight of wet soil.

## Step 4: Place the Bed and Line the Base

Set the frame in position and check it with a level, digging high spots or adding soil under low corners until it sits steady. If burrowing animals are common where you live, staple hardware cloth across the bottom first. Then lay overlapping cardboard over the grass inside the frame. It smothers grass and weeds and breaks down over time, letting roots and worms move freely into the ground below.

## Step 5: Calculate and Mix the Soil

Soil volume is length × width × depth in feet: 8 × 4 × about 1.5 = about 49 cubic feet, or roughly 1.8 cubic yards. That's around 25 bags of 2-cubic-foot mix, so for a bed this size, buying in bulk from a landscape supplier is often cheaper.

| Component | Share | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Quality topsoil or raised-bed soil | About 60% | Body and minerals |
| [Compost](/blog/how-to-compost-at-home/) | About 30% | Nutrients, microbial life, water holding |
| Perlite, pumice or coarse sand | About 10% | Drainage and air |

Fill in layers, mixing as you go and watering each few inches to settle the soil. Expect it to settle by an inch or two over the first weeks, and top it up.

To save soil on deep beds, some gardeners fill the bottom with untreated logs and branches, a method borrowed from hugelkultur. It reduces fill cost, but the wood decomposes and the level sinks over the years.

## Step 6: Plant and Mulch

Water the bed thoroughly and let it settle for a day, then plant. Raised beds suit root crops, greens, tomatoes and herbs, since the soil stays loose. Because the soil warms faster in spring, you can often plant a little earlier, but still watch your last frost date for tender crops. Our [companion planting guide](/blog/companion-planting-guide/) helps plan what to grow together.

Finish with 1–2 inches of mulch, such as straw or shredded leaves, to hold moisture and suppress weeds.

## Watering Raised Beds

Good drainage also means faster drying, especially in hot, windy weather. Check moisture by pushing a finger 2 inches into the soil rather than trusting the surface. Deep, less frequent watering encourages deeper roots than daily sprinkles. A soaker hose or drip line under the mulch is an easy upgrade for consistent watering.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Boards bowing outward | Long span, heavy wet soil | Add a cross brace or stakes at the middle of long sides |
| Soil level dropped | Normal settling and decomposition | Top up with compost each spring |
| Soil dries out fast | Too much sand or no mulch | Add compost; mulch; consider drip irrigation |
| Water pools on top | Heavy, compacted fill | Mix in compost and perlite; avoid walking in the bed |
| Weak plant growth | Low fertility or pH off | Add compost; [test soil pH](/blog/how-to-test-soil-ph/) |
| Grass creeping in | Gaps at the base | Edge along the outside; refresh cardboard or mulch |

## Maintenance Over Time

Top up the bed with 1–2 inches of compost each spring. Rotate crop families from year to year to reduce pests and disease. Cedar will gray naturally in sun and rain; that's cosmetic. Check screws each season and tighten or replace any that have loosened as the wood moves. In fall, cover bare soil with leaves or sow [a cover crop](/blog/fall-cover-crops-for-garden-soil/) to protect and feed it through winter.

## FAQ

### How deep should a raised garden bed be?

Most vegetables do well in 12 inches of loose soil; 18 inches gives more room for carrots and tomatoes and easier reach. Beds built over good native soil can be shallower, since roots grow into the ground below.

### Should I put anything in the bottom of a raised bed?

Cardboard over grass works well and breaks down. Hardware cloth helps if burrowing animals are a problem. Avoid gravel layers, which don't improve drainage and can hold water in the soil above.

### Can I fill a raised bed with just bagged garden soil?

Yes, a raised-bed mix works, but it's more expensive for large beds. Mixing topsoil with compost is usually cheaper and gives you more control.

### Is pressure-treated wood safe for vegetable beds?

Modern treatments are considered much safer than older arsenic-based ones, but opinions still vary. If you want to avoid any doubt, use cedar, redwood, steel or stone, or line treated wood with plastic on the soil side.
