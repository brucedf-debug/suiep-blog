Cracked, yellowed or moldy caulk around a bathtub makes an otherwise clean bathroom look neglected, and a gap in that seam can let water into the wall or subfloor. Recaulking is one of the cheapest bathroom upgrades there is. The bead itself only takes minutes. The results depend on the unglamorous parts: removing every bit of old caulk, getting the joint completely clean and dry, and giving the new caulk time to cure.

**Quick answer:** Cut and scrape out all of the old caulk, clean the joint with rubbing alcohol and let it dry completely. Fill the tub with water so it sits at its lowest position, tape both sides of the joint, and run a continuous bead of 100% silicone or a kitchen-and-bath sealant rated for tubs. Smooth it in one pass, pull the tape right away, and let it cure for the time on the label, often 24 hours or more, before showering.

## Why Caulk Fails

Tub caulk lives with constant humidity, hot-cold swings and small movements every time the tub fills and empties. Even good caulk eventually cracks, pulls away or grows mold, often after a few years. Signs it's time to redo it:

- Black or pink spots that don't clean off
- Gaps, cracks or sections pulling away from the tub or tile
- Yellowing or peeling
- Soft, spongy spots in the wall or floor near the tub (a sign water is getting behind; investigate before recaulking)

## Choose the Right Caulk

| Type | Pros | Cons | Use around a tub? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% silicone (kitchen & bath) | Very flexible, waterproof, mold-resistant additives | Harder to smooth; can't be painted; only sticks to clean surfaces | Yes, the standard choice |
| Hybrid / siliconized polymer (tub & tile) | Easier to tool, some are paintable, good adhesion | Check the label for wet-area rating | Yes, if rated for tubs and showers |
| Siliconized acrylic latex | Easy to smooth, water cleanup | Less flexible; shorter life in wet areas | Only if the label says tub and shower |
| Painter's acrylic caulk | Cheap, paintable | Not for continuously wet areas | No; use it for trim and baseboards |

Choose white or clear to match your tub, and check the cure time; some quick-cure products allow showering sooner.

## What You'll Need

- Caulk and a caulking gun with a smooth, dripless trigger
- Caulk removal tool, plastic scraper or utility knife
- Caulk remover gel for stubborn residue (optional)
- Rubbing alcohol, clean rags and paper towels
- Painter's tape
- Caulk smoothing tool or a gloved finger
- Disposable gloves
- Backer rod for gaps wider than about ¼ inch
- Mold cleaner or diluted bleach (for mold behind old caulk)

## Step 1: Remove All the Old Caulk

Slice along both edges of the old bead with a utility knife or removal tool, holding the blade flat to avoid scratching the tub. Pull it out in strips. Scrape off the remaining film with a plastic scraper; for stubborn silicone residue, apply a caulk remover gel, wait the time on the label, and scrape again. New silicone doesn't bond to old silicone, so every trace needs to go.

## Step 2: Kill Mold and Clean

If there's mold in the joint, treat it with a mold cleaner or a diluted bleach solution, following the product directions and ventilating well. Never mix bleach with vinegar or ammonia. Rinse, then wipe the whole joint with rubbing alcohol to remove soap scum and oils.

## Step 3: Let It Dry Completely

Moisture in the joint is the main reason fresh caulk fails early. Let it dry for several hours, ideally overnight, with the fan running or a window open. A hair dryer on low speeds things up in the corners.

## Step 4: Fill the Tub and Tape

Fill the tub with water. The weight lowers it slightly, opening the joint to its widest point. Caulking now means the bead won't be stretched and cracked the first time someone takes a bath. Leave the water in until the caulk has skinned over.

Run painter's tape along the tub and the wall, leaving a gap the width of the bead you want, usually about ¼ inch. Keep the tape straight; any wobble shows in the finished line.

## Step 5: Apply the Bead

For gaps wider than about ¼ inch, press a foam backer rod into the joint first so the caulk doesn't sink in. Cut the nozzle at a 45-degree angle, slightly smaller than the gap, and puncture the inner seal. Hold the gun at an angle and push the bead along the joint in one steady, continuous motion, moving at a pace that fills the gap completely. Work one wall at a time.

## Step 6: Smooth and Pull the Tape

Immediately smooth the bead with a caulk tool or a gloved fingertip dipped in water (for latex) or soapy water or alcohol (for silicone, check the label). Use one smooth pass per section; repeated passes drag the caulk. Then pull the tape right away, at a low angle away from the bead, before the caulk skins over.

## Step 7: Let It Cure

Leave the water in the tub until the caulk has skinned, then drain it. Don't use the tub or shower until the caulk is fully cured. Many silicones need about 24 hours, and some take longer in cool, humid bathrooms; follow the label. Keep the fan running to help it cure.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| New caulk peeling in weeks | Old residue, soap scum or moisture in the joint | Remove, clean with alcohol, dry fully, redo |
| Bead cracks when tub is filled | Caulked with an empty tub | Redo with the tub filled with water |
| Lumpy, uneven line | Stopping and starting, too much caulk | Smooth right away; practice on cardboard first |
| Caulk sinks into the gap | Gap too wide | Use backer rod, then caulk |
| Mold returns quickly | Mold left behind, poor ventilation | Clean mold thoroughly; run the fan after every shower |
| Tape tore the bead | Pulled after the caulk skinned | Pull tape immediately after smoothing |

## Keep It Clean Longer

- Run the exhaust fan during showers and for 20–30 minutes after.
- Squeegee or wipe the tub edge after showers if mold has been a problem.
- Clean the seam regularly with a bathroom cleaner; avoid abrasive pads that scratch the caulk.
- Check it yearly and patch small gaps before water gets behind.

## FAQ

### Can I put new caulk over old caulk?

It's not recommended. New caulk bonds poorly to old caulk, and any mold or gaps underneath remain. Removing the old bead is the step that makes the job last.

### How long does bathtub caulk last?

It varies with product quality, ventilation and use. Many beads need redoing after several years; poor prep can shorten that to months.

### Why fill the tub before caulking?

A full tub sits slightly lower. Caulking in that position prevents the bead from being stretched and cracked when the tub is used.

### How long until I can shower?

Follow the label. Many silicone caulks need about 24 hours, and some take longer; quick-cure formulas can be ready in a few hours.
