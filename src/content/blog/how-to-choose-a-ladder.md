The wrong ladder isn't just inconvenient — it's one of the more common causes of home DIY injuries. Here's how to pick one that actually fits your projects.

## Step Ladder vs. Extension Ladder

**Step ladders** are self-supporting (no wall needed) and best for indoor work, gutter cleaning, and reaching low rooflines. They come in A-frame form and typically top out around 12-16 feet of reach for homeowner models.

**Extension ladders** lean against a structure and extend telescopically, making them the right choice for second-story exterior work like roof access or high siding repairs.

## Height: Go One Size Bigger Than You Think

The rule of thumb is you should never stand on the top two rungs of a step ladder or the top three rungs of an extension ladder. If your project needs you at 10 feet, buy a ladder rated for meaningfully more than that, not exactly that height.

## Duty Rating Matters More Than People Expect

Every ladder has a weight rating printed on it — this includes your body weight plus tools and materials you're carrying up.

- **Type III (200 lbs)**: light household use
- **Type II (225 lbs)**: medium duty
- **Type I (250 lbs)**: heavy duty, contractor grade
- **Type IA (300 lbs)**: extra heavy duty

When in doubt, size up — a higher duty rating also usually means a sturdier, less flexible ladder even within your actual weight range.

## Material: Aluminum vs. Fiberglass

Aluminum is lighter and cheaper but conducts electricity — never use it near power lines or electrical work. Fiberglass costs more and weighs more but is non-conductive, making it the safer choice for any project near wiring.

## Basic Safety Reminders

Always maintain three points of contact while climbing, set extension ladders at the correct angle (about 75 degrees, or roughly 1 foot of base offset for every 4 feet of height), and never lean or overreach to the side — move the ladder instead.
