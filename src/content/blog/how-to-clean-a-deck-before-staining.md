Stain applied over dirt, mildew or old finish can't soak into the wood, so it peels, blotches or wears through within a season. The stain can gets most of the attention, but cleaning and drying are what decide whether a deck finish lasts years or months. The good news: prep is mostly patience, not skill, and it doesn't require a pressure washer.

**Quick answer:** Clear and sweep the deck, protect nearby plants, and apply an oxygen-based deck cleaner (or a stripper if old stain is peeling). Let it dwell, scrub with the grain, and rinse thoroughly with a hose or a pressure washer on a low setting with a fan tip. Use a wood brightener if the wood looks dark or blotchy, let the deck dry for at least 48 hours, sand any raised grain, and stain only when water soaks in instead of beading.

## What You'll Need

- Stiff-bristle deck brush on a pole (synthetic bristles; avoid wire brushes, which leave metal bits that stain)
- Deck cleaner, and possibly a stain stripper and a wood brightener (see below)
- Garden hose with a spray nozzle, or a pressure washer
- Pump sprayer, roller or garden sprayer for applying cleaner
- Plastic sheeting and tape to protect plants and siding
- Safety glasses, chemical-resistant gloves and old shoes
- Sanding pole or orbital sander with 60–80 grit paper
- Hammer and nail set, or a drill with deck screws

## Does Your Deck Need Cleaning, Stripping or Both?

Start by diagnosing what's on the wood. Choosing the wrong product is the most common reason prep fails.

| Deck condition | What to use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Gray, weathered, no old finish | Oxygen-based deck cleaner | Lifts dirt and gray fibers without harming wood or plants |
| Green or black mildew in shaded spots | Cleaner labeled for mildew and algae | Kills growth instead of just lifting surface dirt |
| Old stain peeling, flaking or patchy | Stain stripper | Cleaners can't dissolve a failing film; new stain won't stick over it |
| Solid or opaque stain in good shape | Cleaner only | Recoat with the same type after cleaning |
| Dark, blotchy or tannin-stained after cleaning | Wood brightener (usually oxalic acid) | Neutralizes cleaner/stripper and restores the natural tone |
| New pressure-treated lumber | Let it weather, then clean | Fresh boards are still wet and often won't accept stain yet |

**Avoid chlorine bleach.** It whitens the surface without cleaning deeply, can break down wood fibers, corrodes metal fasteners and harms plants and lawns.

## Step 1: Pick the Right Day

Clean on a mild, overcast day or when the deck is in shade. Hot sun dries cleaner on the surface before it can work, and dried cleaner leaves residue. Check the forecast too: you need the cleaning day plus at least two dry days afterward, then another dry window for staining.

## Step 2: Clear, Sweep and Inspect

Remove furniture, grills, planters and rugs. Sweep off leaves and grit, and scrape debris from the gaps between boards with a putty knife so water can drain. While you're down there, look for soft or spongy boards, loose railings and raised fasteners. It's better to find those now than after you've stained.

## Step 3: Protect Plants and Surfaces

Soak nearby plants, grass and soil with plain water. Wet foliage absorbs less runoff. Cover plants directly below the deck edge with plastic, but don't leave them covered for hours on a hot day. Tape plastic over siding, doors and anything metal or painted that the cleaner could splash onto.

## Step 4: Apply the Cleaner or Stripper

Mix according to the label, then apply to a manageable section, usually a few boards across the full length so you don't create lap marks. Keep the surface wet for the full dwell time listed on the product, often 10–15 minutes. Mist it lightly with water if it starts to dry. Wear eye protection; strippers in particular are harsh on skin.

## Step 5: Scrub With the Grain

Scrub every board along the grain with the deck brush. Give extra attention to shaded corners, the areas under planters and around stairs, where mildew concentrates. Scrubbing does much of the real cleaning. Rinsing only carries away what the brush has loosened.

## Step 6: Rinse Thoroughly

**With a hose:** A strong spray nozzle is enough for most residential decks. It takes more passes and a little more scrubbing, but carries almost no risk of damaging the wood.

**With a pressure washer:** Use a wide fan tip (25° or 40°, never 0°), keep the wand 8–12 inches from the surface, and move steadily along the grain. Start at low pressure and increase only if needed. Softwoods like cedar and redwood can be damaged at pressures that pressure-treated pine tolerates, so test in an inconspicuous spot first. Lines, fuzzy fibers or gouges mean the pressure is too high or the tip is too close.

Rinse until the water runs clear and no slippery residue remains, then rinse nearby plants again.

## Step 7: Brighten (If Needed)

Cleaners and strippers are alkaline and can leave wood looking dark or blotchy. A wood brightener, usually based on oxalic acid, neutralizes that residue and brings back the natural color, which also helps stain absorb evenly. Apply it to the wet wood, let it dwell as directed, and rinse. It's especially worth using after a stripper and on cedar or redwood.

## Step 8: Let It Dry Completely

This is the step most people rush. Allow at least 48 hours of dry weather before staining, and longer in cool, humid or shaded conditions. Check the stain's label, as some products specify a maximum moisture content.

**The water-drop test:** sprinkle a few drops of water on several boards, including shaded ones. If the water soaks in within a minute or so, the wood is ready. If it beads, the wood is still too wet or has leftover sealer, and needs more drying or another round of stripping.

## Step 9: Sand Raised Grain and Fix Problems

Washing raises wood fibers, and stain over fuzzy or splintered boards looks rough. Lightly sand problem areas with 60–80 grit on a pole sander or orbital sander, then sweep or blow off the dust. Avoid very fine grits: sanding too smooth closes the wood's pores and can reduce how much stain it absorbs.

Reset popped nails with a nail set, or replace them with deck screws. Replace any soft, rotten boards. Stain won't fix structural problems, and a new board is easier to install now than after the finish is on.

## Troubleshooting Common Prep Problems

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Fuzzy, furry wood after washing | Pressure too high or tip too close | Let dry, then sand lightly with 60–80 grit |
| Gouges or stripes along boards | Narrow tip or stopping the wand in one spot | Sand smooth; use a fan tip and keep moving |
| Wood looks darker after cleaning | Tannins or alkaline cleaner residue | Apply wood brightener and rinse |
| Water still beads after drying | Old sealer or stain left on the surface | Strip, rinse, brighten, dry again |
| White residue on boards | Cleaner dried in sun or wasn't rinsed fully | Rewet, scrub and rinse thoroughly |
| Mildew returns within weeks | Growth not fully killed; damp, shaded deck | Use a mildew-rated cleaner; trim plants for airflow |
| Brown or yellow plants below the deck | Cleaner runoff | Water plants heavily before and after; cover them during cleaning |

## Timing the Stain Itself

Once the deck passes the water-drop test, stain during a stretch of mild, dry weather. Avoid midday sun on the boards, which makes stain dry too fast and leave lap marks. Plan for at least a day or two without rain after application so the finish can cure. Check the product's temperature range, since many don't cure well below about 50°F (10°C).

## FAQ

### Can I clean a deck without a pressure washer?

Yes. A good cleaner, a stiff brush and a garden hose with a strong nozzle clean most decks well. It takes longer, but there's almost no risk of gouging the wood.

### How long should a deck dry before staining?

Plan on at least 48 hours of dry weather, and more in cool, humid or shaded conditions. The water-drop test is more reliable than the calendar.

### How soon can I stain a new pressure-treated deck?

New pressure-treated lumber is usually too wet to accept stain right away. Many manufacturers suggest waiting several weeks to a few months, then cleaning and doing the water-drop test before staining. Follow the lumber and stain makers' guidance.

### Do I have to remove all the old stain?

Not always. If the old stain is the same type and still sound, cleaning is usually enough. If it's peeling, flaking or patchy, strip it, because new stain will fail along with the old layer beneath it.
