Nail holes, doorknob dents and the occasional bigger hole are among the most common home repairs, and among the easiest to get wrong by rushing. A patch rarely fails because of the method. It shows because the compound wasn't feathered wide enough, wasn't sanded flat, or wasn't primed before painting. Take those steps slowly and the repair disappears.

**Quick answer:** Fill nail holes and small dents with lightweight spackle. Cover holes up to about 6 inches with a self-adhesive mesh or metal patch and two or three thin coats of joint compound, feathered several inches beyond the hole. For bigger holes, cut out a square, add wood backing, screw in a new piece of drywall, tape and mud the seams. Sand smooth between coats, match any texture, prime the patch, then paint.

## Match the Method to the Hole

| Damage | Method | Coats | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nail and screw holes, small dents | Lightweight spackle, putty knife | 1–2 | Under an hour plus drying |
| Popped drywall screw or nail | Drive a new screw beside it, sink old one, fill | 2 | A few hours with drying |
| Holes ½–6 in (doorknobs, anchors) | Self-adhesive mesh or metal patch + joint compound | 2–3 | 1–2 days with drying |
| Holes over 6 in | Cut to a square, add backing, new drywall piece, tape and mud | 3 | 2–3 days with drying |
| Cracks at seams or corners | Remove loose tape, re-tape, mud | 2–3 | 1–2 days |

## What You'll Need

- Lightweight spackle for small holes
- Joint compound (premixed all-purpose, or lightweight topping for final coats)
- Self-adhesive fiberglass mesh or metal patch
- For large holes: scrap drywall of the same thickness, 1x3 or 1x4 wood strips, drywall screws, paper or mesh tape
- 4 in and 6 in putty or taping knives (a 10 in knife helps on big patches)
- Utility knife, drywall saw
- 120–220 grit sanding sponge or pole sander
- Primer and matching paint
- Work light, drop cloth, damp sponge

## Small Holes and Dents

1. Brush away loose bits and press down any raised edges with the knife handle.
2. Press spackle into the hole with a putty knife, then scrape the surface flat with the knife held at a low angle.
3. Let it dry. Lightweight spackle often shrinks slightly; add a second skim if there's a dip.
4. Sand lightly with a fine sponge until flush.

## Medium Holes (½ to 6 Inches)

**Step 1: Clean the edges.** Cut away torn paper and crumbling gypsum with a utility knife. Loose paper edges bubble under compound.

**Step 2: Apply the patch.** Center a self-adhesive mesh or metal patch over the hole, pressing it flat. The patch should overlap solid wall by at least an inch on every side.

**Step 3: First coat.** Spread a thin coat of joint compound over the patch with a 6 in knife, pressing it through the mesh. Feather the edges out 2–3 inches past the patch so there's no ridge. Don't try to hide the patch completely on the first coat; a thin coat dries faster and cracks less.

![A hand spreading joint compound over a drywall patch with a putty knife](../../assets/blog/drywall-patch-closeup.jpg)

**Step 4: Dry, then sand lightly.** Drying time depends on thickness, humidity and the product. Premixed compound often needs overnight; setting-type powder compound hardens in a set time listed on the bag. Knock down ridges with a sanding sponge.

**Step 5: Second and third coats.** Apply each coat a little wider, 2 inches farther than the last, using a wider knife. Wider, thinner coats are what make a patch invisible. Sand after the final coat until smooth.

## Large Holes (Over 6 Inches)

1. **Check behind the wall** for wires and pipes before cutting.
2. **Cut the damage into a clean square or rectangle** with a drywall saw.
3. **Add backing:** screw wood strips inside the hole, behind the existing drywall on two sides, with drywall screws.
4. **Cut a new drywall piece** to fit the opening, matching the wall's thickness, and screw it to the backing strips.
5. **Tape the seams** with paper tape bedded in compound, or mesh tape, then apply two or three progressively wider coats, sanding between them.

A dinner-plate-sized hole is still a manageable DIY job with this method. Call a pro for water-damaged drywall, sagging ceilings, or holes near electrical boxes you're unsure about.

## Sanding Without Making a Mess

- Sand only after compound is fully dry. Wet compound clogs sandpaper and tears.
- Use light pressure; drywall paper fuzzes if sanded through.
- Hold a work light at a low angle across the wall to reveal high and low spots.
- A damp sponge can smooth small areas with far less dust.
- Wear a dust mask, and close doors to contain dust.

## Match the Wall Texture

A perfectly smooth patch stands out on a textured wall. Aerosol texture products for orange peel and knockdown are available; practice on cardboard to dial in distance and spray pattern first. For light orange peel, dabbing thinned compound with a sponge or roller can blend well. Let texture dry fully before priming.

## Prime, Then Paint

Joint compound absorbs paint differently from painted wall, so an unprimed patch often shows as a dull or shiny spot ("flashing"), even with the same paint. Prime the patch with a drywall primer and let it dry.

For the best result, repaint the wall corner to corner. Spot touch-ups can work on flat paint in low-light areas, but satin and semi-gloss usually show a halo. Use the same paint and sheen, and a roller rather than a brush to match the wall's texture. Our guide to [painting a room](/blog/how-to-paint-a-room/) covers the full process.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Patch outline visible after painting | Not feathered wide enough, or unprimed | Add a wider skim coat, sand, prime, repaint |
| Compound cracked | Coat too thick, or dried too fast | Scrape loose bits; apply thinner coats |
| Bubbles in compound | Loose paper facing underneath | Cut away loose paper, seal with primer, recoat |
| Hump over the patch | Metal patch or tape too thick, not feathered | Feather compound much wider to blend |
| Dent after drying | Compound shrank | Apply another thin coat |
| Patch looks shinier or duller | Spot touch-up or no primer | Prime and repaint the whole wall |
| Screw heads popping through | Screws too shallow or framing moved | Drive them slightly below the surface; fill |

## Cleanup and Storage

Clean knives and pans before compound dries. For leftover premixed compound, smooth the surface flat, pour a thin layer of water on top and seal the lid; pour off the water before the next use. Keep compound from freezing.

## FAQ

### Spackle or joint compound?

Spackle is quick for small holes and dents. Joint compound is better for larger patches, taped seams and feathering wide areas.

### How long does joint compound take to dry?

Premixed compound often needs about 24 hours per coat, depending on thickness and humidity. Setting-type powder compounds harden in the time printed on the bag and allow faster recoats.

### Can I paint the same day?

Only when the final coat is completely dry and sanded, and after priming. With quick-setting compound and thin coats, same-day finishing is possible on small repairs.

### How do I patch a hole from a wall anchor?

Pull or push the anchor in, fill with spackle or compound, and use a small mesh patch if the hole is larger than about half an inch. When you rehang the shelf, use a stud if possible; see our [floating shelf guide](/blog/diy-floating-shelf/).
