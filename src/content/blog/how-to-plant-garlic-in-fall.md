Garlic is one of the few vegetables that does best when planted in fall. You set individual cloves in the ground a few weeks before it freezes, they grow roots and maybe a little green before winter, then rest under mulch until spring gives them the season they need to swell into full bulbs. It's a low-effort crop for most of its long life, but most of the decisions that decide bulb size happen in these few weeks of fall.

**Quick answer:** Plant large cloves from certified seed garlic about 2–4 weeks before the ground usually freezes. Set them pointy end up, 2 inches deep, 4–6 inches apart, in loose, well-drained soil enriched with compost. Water in, then mulch with 4–6 inches of straw or shredded leaves. In spring, keep weeds down, water in dry spells, snap off hardneck scapes, and harvest when about half the leaves have browned.

## Why Fall Planting Beats Spring

Garlic needs a period of cold, usually several weeks of soil below about 40–50°F (4–10°C), to trigger the clove to divide into a bulb. Spring-planted garlic often misses that cold and produces small bulbs or single round "rounds" that never split into cloves. Fall planting also gives roots a head start in cool, workable soil, so the plant takes off as soon as the ground warms in spring.

## Hardneck or Softneck?

| | Hardneck | Softneck |
|---|---|---|
| **Climate** | Best in cold winters | More forgiving in mild winters |
| **Cloves** | Fewer, larger, easy to peel | More, smaller, in several layers |
| **Flavor** | Often more complex and pungent | Usually milder |
| **Scapes** | Yes: a curly, edible flower stalk in early summer | No |
| **Storage** | About 4–6 months | Often 6–9 months or longer |
| **Braiding** | No (stiff stalk) | Yes |

Many gardeners grow some of each: hardnecks for flavor and scapes, softnecks for long storage.

## Step 1: Time the Planting

Aim for 2–4 weeks before the ground usually freezes solid. That's late enough that cloves won't push much top growth before winter, but early enough for roots to establish. In cold regions, that's often early to mid fall; in milder areas, late fall or even early winter. A few inches of green growth before winter is normal and harmless. Your local extension service or regional planting calendar is a better guide than a national date.

## Step 2: Get the Right Garlic

Buy seed garlic from a garden center or seed supplier. Grocery store garlic may be a variety suited to a different climate, may carry soil-borne diseases, and some is treated to slow sprouting. Seed garlic is sold in bulbs; one pound yields roughly 30–50 plantable cloves depending on variety, enough for a short bed.

Just before planting, break bulbs into cloves, leaving the papery skin on each one. Plant only the biggest, firmest cloves. Clove size is one of the strongest predictors of bulb size at harvest. Keep the small ones for the kitchen.

## Step 3: Prepare the Bed

Garlic wants full sun and loose, well-drained soil rich in organic matter. Work 1–2 inches of compost into the top several inches. Avoid spots where water collects in winter; wet soil rots cloves. On heavy clay, a [raised bed](/blog/how-to-build-a-raised-garden-bed/) makes a big difference. Garlic prefers a near-neutral pH, so a [soil test](/blog/how-to-test-soil-ph/) is worth doing if you've never checked. Rotate: don't plant where onions, leeks or garlic grew in the last few years.

## Step 4: Plant at the Right Depth and Spacing

| Detail | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Orientation | Pointy end up, flat root end down |
| Depth | About 2 in of soil over the tip; a bit deeper in very cold regions |
| Spacing in the row | 4–6 in |
| Spacing between rows | 8–12 in |
| Wide beds | Grid of about 6 × 6 in |

Push each clove in or set it in a furrow, cover, and firm the soil gently. Water the bed well to settle it.

## Step 5: Mulch for Winter

After the soil cools, cover the bed with 4–6 inches of straw (not hay, which carries seeds) or shredded leaves. Mulch evens out freeze-thaw swings that can push cloves up out of the soil, holds moisture and suppresses spring weeds, which matters because garlic competes poorly with them. In mild climates, a thinner layer is enough. If you have piles of [fall leaves](/blog/how-to-rake-leaves-efficiently/), shred them first so they don't mat.

## Spring and Early Summer Care

- **Pull mulch back slightly** if shoots struggle to push through; leave a thinner layer for weeds and moisture.
- **Weed regularly.** Garlic has shallow roots and hates competition.
- **Water during dry spells,** about an inch a week while bulbs are sizing up in late spring. Taper off a couple of weeks before harvest so bulbs don't rot or split.
- **Feed lightly** in early spring with a nitrogen-rich fertilizer or compost if growth looks pale. Stop feeding once bulbs start swelling.
- **Snap off hardneck scapes** when they curl, which sends more energy into the bulb. Scapes are delicious in pesto and stir-fries.

## When and How to Harvest

Garlic is usually ready in early to mid summer, often 8–9 months after fall planting. The best sign is the leaves: each green leaf corresponds to a papery wrapper around the bulb. Harvest when roughly the lower third to half of the leaves have browned while the top leaves are still green.

Loosen the soil with a garden fork a few inches away from the plant, then lift. Don't yank by the stalk, and don't wash the bulbs.

## Curing and Storing

Cure garlic in a warm, dry, shaded spot with good airflow, such as a garage or covered porch, for about 2–4 weeks. Hang it in bundles or spread it on a rack. Once the wrappers are papery and the necks are dry and tight, trim roots and stalks (or braid softnecks) and store in a cool, dry, dark place. Save the biggest bulbs to replant next fall.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Small bulbs | Small cloves, weeds, poor soil or spring planting | Plant big cloves in fall; weed; add compost |
| Single, unsplit bulbs ("rounds") | Not enough cold, or tiny cloves | Plant earlier in fall; use larger cloves |
| Cloves heaved out of the ground | Freeze-thaw cycles, shallow planting | Plant 2 in deep; mulch after soil cools |
| Rotted cloves | Soggy winter soil | Improve drainage; use raised beds |
| Yellow tips in spring | Cold damage, lack of nitrogen or water stress | Feed lightly; water in dry spells |
| Bulb wrappers split or cloves spread apart | Harvested too late | Dig when half the leaves are still green |

## FAQ

### Can I plant garlic from the grocery store?

It sometimes grows, but it may be the wrong type for your climate and can bring disease into your soil. Seed garlic from a reputable supplier is more reliable.

### Is it too late to plant if the ground is cold?

If you can still work the soil, plant. Late-planted garlic may produce smaller bulbs but usually still grows well.

### Can I grow garlic in containers?

Yes, in a deep container (at least 8–10 in) with drainage holes and good potting mix. Protect containers from hard freezes in cold regions.

### What should I plant after harvesting garlic?

The bed is free by midsummer, so sow a quick crop like beans or fall greens, or a [cover crop](/blog/fall-cover-crops-for-garden-soil/) to rebuild the soil.
