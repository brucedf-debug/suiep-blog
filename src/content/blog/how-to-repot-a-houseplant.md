Repotting stresses a plant no matter how gently you do it. That's why so many houseplants struggle after being moved into a bigger pot "just in case." The goal isn't to repot on a schedule; it's to notice when the roots have truly run out of room or the soil has worn out, then make the move with as little disruption as possible.

**Quick answer:** Repot when roots circle the pot or poke out of the drainage holes, water runs straight through, or the plant dries out far faster than it used to. Do it in spring or early summer if you can. Choose a pot only 1–2 inches wider with drainage holes, water the day before, loosen circling roots, set the plant at the same depth, and fill with fresh mix suited to that plant. Water well, keep it out of harsh sun for a week, and wait about a month to fertilize.

## Signs Your Plant Needs Repotting

| Sign | What it means |
|---|---|
| Roots coming out of drainage holes | Roots have filled the pot |
| Roots circling on the surface or in a tight mat | Root-bound |
| Water runs straight through without soaking in | Soil is mostly roots or has become water-repellent |
| Plant dries out in a day or two | Too little soil for the root mass |
| Growth has stalled despite good light and care | Roots may have no room left |
| Plant is top-heavy and tips over | Pot too small for the plant |
| Old, crusty, broken-down soil | Soil needs refreshing, even if the pot is fine |

If none of these apply, leave it alone. Many houseplants only need repotting every one to two years, and slow growers even less. A plant you just brought home usually doesn't need an immediate repot unless it's clearly root-bound; let it adjust to your home for a few weeks first.

## When to Repot

Spring and early summer are best, when most plants are actively growing and recover quickly. In late fall and winter, growth slows and a disturbed plant takes longer to heal. If a plant is in real trouble, such as severe root rot or a pot that's cracked, repot right away rather than waiting.

## Choosing the Pot

- **Size up by 1–2 inches in diameter** for small and medium plants, 2–4 inches for large floor plants. A much bigger pot holds wet soil the roots can't reach, which invites rot.
- **Drainage holes are essential.** Use a decorative pot without holes only as an outer cover, with the plant in a draining nursery pot inside.
- **Material matters:** terracotta breathes and dries faster (good for succulents and plants prone to overwatering); plastic and glazed ceramic hold moisture longer.

## Choosing the Right Soil

| Plant type | Mix |
|---|---|
| Most tropical foliage plants (pothos, philodendron, monstera) | Quality indoor potting mix, often with added perlite or bark |
| Succulents and cacti | Fast-draining cactus mix, extra perlite or pumice |
| Orchids (epiphytic types) | Chunky orchid bark mix, not soil |
| Aroids that like airy roots | Potting mix with bark and perlite, often a third each |
| Ferns and calatheas | Moisture-holding mix with peat or coco coir plus perlite |

Don't use garden soil in pots. It compacts, drains poorly and can bring in pests. Skip a gravel layer at the bottom too; it doesn't improve drainage and actually raises the zone of wet soil in the pot.

## Step 1: Water the Day Before

Water about a day ahead. Lightly moist soil holds together around the roots and slides out more easily. Bone-dry soil crumbles and tears fine roots, while freshly soaked soil is heavy and messy.

## Step 2: Remove the Plant

Tip the pot on its side, support the base of the plant between your fingers, and squeeze or tap the pot to loosen it. Ease the plant out. Never pull by the stem. For a stuck plant, run a butter knife around the inside edge. Cut a plastic nursery pot away if necessary.

## Step 3: Inspect and Loosen the Roots

Healthy roots are firm and white, tan or light-colored. Rotted roots are dark, mushy and may smell sour; trim them off with clean scissors.

Gently tease apart roots circling the outside of the root ball so they grow outward into the new soil. For a dense, matted root ball, make three or four shallow vertical cuts down the sides with a clean knife. It looks harsh, but it encourages new roots.

## Step 4: Position and Fill

Add fresh mix to the bottom of the new pot so the top of the root ball sits about 1 inch below the rim, at the same depth the plant grew before. Burying the stem deeper invites rot in many plants. Fill around the sides, tapping the pot and pressing lightly to remove large air pockets without packing the mix hard.

## Step 5: Water and Let It Rest

Water thoroughly until it drains out the bottom, then top up any soil that settles. Put the plant back in its usual spot, or in slightly softer light for about a week if it was in strong sun. Wait about a month before [fertilizing](/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants/); fresh mix usually contains nutrients, and tender new roots are easily burned by fertilizer salts.

## Handling Transplant Shock

Some drooping, a yellow leaf or two, or a pause in growth over the next week or two is normal. Don't respond by watering more; the new soil holds more moisture than the old, root-packed pot did. Check the soil before each watering and give the plant time. New growth is the clearest sign it has settled in.

| Symptom after repotting | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Wilting for a few days | Root disturbance | Keep out of harsh sun; don't overwater |
| Yellowing lower leaves | Shock or soggy soil | Let the top inch dry before watering |
| Soil stays wet for a week or more | Pot too big or mix too dense | Water less; repot into smaller pot or airier mix if it persists |
| Tiny flies around the soil | Fungus gnats from moist fresh mix | Let the surface dry; see our [fungus gnat guide](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) |
| No new growth after a month or more | Low light, wrong mix or season | Check light and mix; be patient in winter |

## Refreshing Soil Without Upsizing

If a plant has reached the size you want, you can keep it in the same pot. Remove it, trim back about a quarter of the outer roots, tease out old soil, and repot in the same container with fresh mix. Alternatively, scrape off the top inch or two of old soil and replace it with fresh mix ("top-dressing"), which works well for large floor plants.

## FAQ

### How often should houseplants be repotted?

Fast growers may need it yearly; many plants go two years or more. Watch for the signs above rather than following a fixed schedule.

### Can I repot a plant in winter?

Yes, if it's an emergency like root rot or a broken pot. Otherwise, waiting until spring gives the plant a faster recovery.

### Should I water right after repotting?

Usually yes, thoroughly, to settle the soil. Succulents and cacti are an exception: many growers wait several days to a week so any damaged roots can heal first.

### Is it a good time to divide or take cuttings?

Repotting is a good moment to divide plants that grow in clumps, or to take stem cuttings. See our guide to [propagating houseplants from cuttings](/blog/propagate-houseplants-from-cuttings/).
