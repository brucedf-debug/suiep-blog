Soil pH controls how available nutrients are to plant roots — even perfectly fertilized soil underperforms if the pH is too far off for what you're growing. Most vegetables and ornamentals want a slightly acidic to neutral range, roughly 6.0 to 7.0.

## Why pH Matters More Than Most Gardeners Realize

At the wrong pH, nutrients can be present in the soil but chemically locked in a form roots can't absorb. This is why a plant can show classic nutrient-deficiency symptoms — yellowing leaves, stunted growth — even in soil that's technically well-fertilized.

## Step 1: Test Your Soil

A basic home test kit (strips or a liquid solution) gives a reasonable read for most garden purposes.

![Hands holding a scoop of healthy garden soil with a small seedling](../../assets/blog/soil-ph-test.jpg)

For a more precise reading, a local agricultural extension office will test a soil sample for a small fee and often includes nutrient analysis too.

## Step 2: Understand What You're Growing

- **Acid-loving plants** (blueberries, azaleas, rhododendrons): prefer pH 4.5-5.5
- **Most vegetables and lawns**: prefer pH 6.0-7.0
- **Lavender, clematis**: tolerate more alkaline soil, up to pH 7.5-8.0

## Step 3: Raise pH (Make Soil Less Acidic)

Work garden lime into the soil in fall or several weeks before planting — it takes time to fully react. Wood ash also raises pH gradually and adds potassium as a bonus.

## Step 4: Lower pH (Make Soil More Acidic)

Elemental sulfur is the most reliable long-term fix, though it acts slowly (weeks to months). For a faster, temporary effect, used coffee grounds or pine needle mulch add mild acidity as they break down.

## Retest Before Assuming It Worked

pH amendments take time to fully react with the soil. Retest 4-8 weeks after applying lime or sulfur before adding more — overcorrecting is a common mistake that swings the pH too far the other direction.
