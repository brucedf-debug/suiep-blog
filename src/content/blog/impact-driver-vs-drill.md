An impact driver and a drill/driver look almost identical, and many first-time buyers assume they do the same job. They don't. Knowing how each one delivers force helps you avoid stripped screws, snapped bits and buying a tool you didn't need. This guide explains the mechanics and the practical trade-offs using manufacturer specifications and how the tools are designed, rather than tests of specific models.

**Quick answer:** A drill/driver spins with steady, adjustable torque and has a clutch, so it's best for drilling holes and driving small or delicate screws with control. An impact driver adds rapid rotational hammer blows when it meets resistance, so it drives long screws and lag bolts quickly with less effort and wrist strain. If you only buy one, get a drill/driver. Add an impact driver for decks, fences, framing and other heavy fastening.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| | Drill/driver | Impact driver |
|---|---|---|
| **How it delivers force** | Continuous rotation | Rotation plus rapid rotational impacts under load |
| **Chuck** | Keyless chuck (⅜ or ½ in) for round or hex bits | ¼ in hex quick-release; hex-shank bits only |
| **Clutch** | Yes: adjustable, prevents overdriving | Usually no clutch; some have speed or mode settings |
| **Torque** | Moderate, steady | High, delivered in pulses |
| **Size and weight** | Larger for its power | Compact and light for its power |
| **Noise** | Quiet hum | Loud rattling when impacting |
| **Kickback into the wrist** | Can twist hard if the bit binds | Much less; impacts absorb reaction |
| **Best at** | Drilling holes, small screws, precise work | Long screws, lag bolts, repetitive fastening |

## How a Drill Works

A drill turns the chuck with steady force. The clutch, the numbered collar behind the chuck, slips at a set torque, so screws stop at the right depth instead of stripping or sinking too far. That control makes a drill ideal for cabinet hardware, hinges, furniture assembly and drilling clean holes in wood, metal and, with a hammer-drill mode and masonry bit, brick and concrete. See our [cordless drill buying guide](/blog/cordless-drill-buying-guide/) for features to look for.

## How an Impact Driver Works

An impact driver spins normally until the fastener resists, then an internal hammer and anvil deliver rapid rotational blows, often thousands per minute. Those bursts drive a 3-inch deck screw or lag bolt in seconds with little strain on your wrist, because the impacts take the reaction force that would otherwise twist your arm. The same mechanism makes impact drivers compact for the torque they deliver and gives them their distinctive rattle, which is normal.

## Which Tool for Which Job

| Task | Better tool | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Drilling precise holes in wood or metal | Drill | Smooth control, round-shank bits |
| Drilling into masonry | Hammer drill | Hammering action along the bit's axis |
| Cabinet hinges, small hardware | Drill | Clutch prevents stripping |
| Furniture assembly | Drill (low clutch setting) | Avoids overtightening into particleboard |
| Deck and fence screws | Impact driver | Fast, no stalling, less wrist strain; see our [fence guide](/blog/how-to-build-a-wood-fence/) |
| Lag screws and structural fasteners | Impact driver | High torque |
| Removing stuck or rusted screws | Impact driver | Impacts break them loose |
| Mixing paint or thin-set | Drill (½ in chuck, low speed) | Continuous torque |
| Large hole saws and spade bits | Drill | Steady rotation, side handle for control |
| Building a [workbench](/blog/diy-garage-workbench/) or shelves | Both | Drill for pilot holes, impact for screws |

## Bits and Accessories

- **Impact drivers take only ¼-inch hex-shank bits.** Round-shank drill bits won't fit without a hex adapter.
- **Use impact-rated bits** in an impact driver. Standard driver bits are more brittle and can snap under repeated impacts.
- **Hex-shank drill bits** let an impact driver drill small holes in a pinch, though with less control.
- **Drills accept both** round and hex bits, and larger bits up to their chuck size.

## Do You Need Both?

| Your projects | What to buy |
|---|---|
| Hanging pictures, assembling furniture, small repairs | Drill/driver only |
| Occasional DIY builds with 2x4s | Drill/driver; add an impact driver if you do many screws |
| Decks, fences, framing, frequent builds | Both |
| Auto or equipment work | Impact driver or impact wrench, plus a drill |

Many [combo kits](/blog/best-budget-power-tool-combo-kits/) bundle both on one battery platform, which often costs less than buying them separately. With both, you can drill pilot holes with the drill and drive screws with the impact driver without constantly changing bits.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using a drill's clutch on long deck screws.** It either slips before the screw seats or twists your wrist when set high. Use an impact driver.
- **Using an impact driver for delicate hardware.** Its bursts can snap small screws or strip soft materials. Use a drill with the clutch set low.
- **Non-impact-rated bits in an impact driver.** They wear quickly or break.
- **Skipping pilot holes in hardwood or near board ends.** Even with high torque, wood can split.
- **Judging by voltage alone.** Torque, speed, motor type and mechanism design matter as much as the battery rating.

## FAQ

### Can an impact driver strip a screw?

Yes, especially with worn or non-impact-rated bits or at full speed on small screws. Use the right bit size, keep pressure straight on the screw, and start slowly.

### Is an impact driver louder than a drill?

Yes. The rattling sound comes from the impact mechanism and is normal. Hearing protection is a good idea for long sessions.

### Can I drill holes with an impact driver?

For small holes with hex-shank bits, yes. For precise, clean or larger holes, a drill works better.

### Is an impact driver the same as an impact wrench?

No. Impact wrenches have a square drive for sockets and much higher torque for nuts and bolts. Impact drivers take hex bits and are meant for screws and smaller fasteners.
