Fall storms and winter ice are the two most common reasons a house loses power for more than a few hours, and both tend to hit right when you're least prepared for it. A generator sitting unused in the garage does nothing for you at 2 a.m. when the furnace stops and the freezer starts warming up — the time to figure out sizing, fuel type, and safe setup is before the forecast turns ugly, not during the outage. Here's how to sort through the options so you buy a generator that actually covers what you need instead of one that just looked impressive on the shelf tag.

## Figure Out Your Actual Wattage Needs First

The single biggest mistake in generator shopping is picking a size based on price or gut feeling instead of doing the math. Start by listing what you actually want to run during an outage — a refrigerator, a sump pump, a [furnace](/blog/get-furnace-ready-for-winter/) blower motor, some lights, maybe a window air conditioner — and add up their running watts. Then account for starting watts, which is the surge a motor-driven appliance draws for a second or two when it first kicks on; a refrigerator or sump pump can spike to two or three times its running wattage at startup, and that surge is what actually determines the minimum size generator you need, not the steady-state total. A generator in the 3,000 to 5,000-watt range covers a well-chosen set of essentials for most homes, while whole-house coverage with central air and an electric range pushes you well into standby-generator territory, which is a different category of purchase entirely with permanent installation and an automatic transfer switch.

## Portable vs. Inverter vs. Standby: Picking the Right Category

A conventional portable generator runs its engine at a constant speed regardless of load, which makes it louder and less fuel-efficient but generally cheaper per watt of output — a reasonable choice if you mainly need to power a handful of large appliances and noise isn't a major concern. An inverter generator adjusts engine speed to match the actual load, which makes it quieter, more fuel-efficient, and produces cleaner power that's safe for sensitive electronics like laptops and medical equipment; the tradeoff is a higher price for the same wattage. Standby generators are installed permanently outside the house, run on natural gas or propane piped in, and start automatically within seconds of an outage — they cost far more and typically need professional installation, but they remove the need to haul anything outside or refuel manually during a storm. For most homeowners who want backup power without a major installation project, a portable or inverter model is the practical starting point.

## Gas, Propane, or Dual-Fuel: Weighing the Fuel Question

Gasoline is the most common fuel for portable generators and the easiest to find in normal times, but it degrades in storage over months and can be the hardest fuel to source during a widespread outage when gas stations lose power too. Propane stores indefinitely without going stale, which makes it appealing for a generator that might sit unused for a year between real emergencies, though propane models typically deliver somewhat less power per tank size than gasoline. Dual-fuel generators run on either gasoline or propane and let you switch depending on what's available, which is genuinely useful flexibility if you're building an emergency setup rather than just grabbing whatever's cheapest. Whichever fuel you choose, store it properly and rotate gasoline stock periodically — stale fuel sitting in a generator's tank or carburetor over winter is one of the most common reasons a generator won't start when you actually need it.

## Running It Safely: Carbon Monoxide Is the Real Danger

More people are hurt by generator misuse than by the storms that made them need one in the first place, and nearly all of that risk comes down to carbon monoxide. Never run a generator inside a garage, basement, porch, or any enclosed or partially enclosed space, even with the door open — carbon monoxide can build up to lethal levels within minutes and doesn't announce itself with any smell. Generators need to run outdoors, well away from windows, doors, and vents, with the exhaust pointed away from the house. Install carbon monoxide detectors on every level of the home if you don't already have them, and treat a generator-specific model with a built-in low-oxygen shutoff sensor as a real safety feature worth paying for, not a marketing gimmick. Beyond carbon monoxide, never plug a generator directly into a wall outlet to power household circuits unless it's connected through a properly installed transfer switch — backfeeding power into your home's wiring this way can electrocute utility workers repairing lines outside, and it's illegal in most places for exactly that reason.

## Sizing Your Extension Cords and Connections Correctly

A generator is only as useful as the cords carrying power from it to whatever you're running, and undersized cords are a common way people lose performance or create a fire hazard without realizing it. Use heavy-duty outdoor-rated extension cords sized for the wattage you're drawing — a cord too thin for the load will heat up, waste power, and can melt its insulation under sustained use. Keep cord runs as short as practical, since voltage drop increases with distance, and never run cords through standing water or under rugs where they can overheat or trip a hazard. If you're planning to power specific circuits like a furnace blower or well pump rather than plugging in individual appliances, a transfer switch installed by a licensed electrician is the safer and more convenient route, letting you power selected circuits directly from your breaker panel instead of running extension cords through windows and doorways.

## Maintenance That Keeps It Ready When You Need It

A generator that hasn't been started in a year is a gamble, not a safety net. Run it for fifteen to twenty minutes every month or two, ideally under some load, so the engine, carburetor, and starter stay in working order rather than seizing up from disuse. Change the oil on the schedule in the owner's manual — most small engines need their first oil change much sooner than people expect, often within the first 20 to 25 hours of use. Keep the air filter clean and spark plug in good condition, and if you're storing gasoline in the unit between uses, add a fuel stabilizer to keep it from gumming up the carburetor. None of this takes long, but skipping it is exactly how a generator that looked fine in the garage turns into one that won't start during the first cold snap of the season.
