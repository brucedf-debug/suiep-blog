A plant that looked fine two weeks ago suddenly has leaves dusted with tiny pale specks, like someone flicked chalk dust across them. Nothing is crawling on top where you'd expect to see it, and by the time you notice fine threads stretched between the stems, the infestation is usually well established. Spider mites are the most commonly overlooked houseplant pest, not because they're rare, but because they're almost invisible until the damage is already visible, and the conditions that cause them — dry air, heated rooms, stressed plants — are exactly the conditions most homes have right now.

**Quick answer:** Spider mites are tiny arachnids, not insects, and they thrive in the warm, dry air that heating systems create indoors in fall and winter. Confirm you have them by tapping a suspect leaf over white paper and watching for moving specks, or by checking leaf undersides and stem joints for fine webbing. Treat by isolating the plant, rinsing it thoroughly, and applying insecticidal soap or neem oil to every leaf surface — especially the undersides — every five to seven days for three full treatment cycles, because a single spray only kills the mites present that day, not the eggs that hatch afterward. Raising humidity around the plant afterward is what keeps them from coming right back.

## What Spider Mites Actually Look Like

Spider mites are arachnids, closely related to spiders rather than insects, and most of the common houseplant species are barely visible without help. Adults are about the size of a period on this page — roughly 0.5 millimeters — and range from pale yellow-green to reddish-brown depending on the species and how recently they've fed. You won't reliably spot an individual mite just by glancing at a leaf; you're looking for what they leave behind.

The first sign is usually stippling: tiny white or yellow dots scattered across the upper surface of a leaf, where the mite has pierced a cell from the underside and drained it. On a plant with many leaves, this can look like a dusty, bleached-out patchiness before you register that it's pest damage rather than a light or watering problem. As the infestation progresses, leaves take on a bronzed or silvery cast, then yellow fully and drop. Fine, silky webbing stretched across leaf axils, along stems, or between the undersides of leaves is the clearest confirmation — mites spin it to move between feeding sites and to protect their eggs, and by the time you see noticeable webbing, the population is already large.

To check a plant you suspect is affected, hold a sheet of white paper or a white plate under a leaf and tap the leaf sharply a few times. Tiny specks that fall onto the paper and then start moving — rather than just sitting there — are mites. A 10x magnifying loupe makes this easier and lets you see whether they're moving quickly in a line (often a sign of a heavier, more mobile population) or just a few scattered individuals.

## Why Winter Is Spider Mite Season Indoors

Spider mites reproduce fastest in warm, dry conditions, and a heated home in late fall and winter is close to ideal for them: indoor relative humidity often drops below 30% once furnaces and radiators run regularly, while temperatures stay in the comfortable 68–72°F range they prefer. A female spider mite can lay several eggs a day, and in warm, dry air those eggs can hatch in as little as three days, with a full generation completing in one to two weeks. That's fast enough for a population to go from unnoticeable to visibly damaging within a month, especially on a plant that's also dealing with lower light and less frequent watering as part of its normal seasonal slowdown — a stressed plant is a weaker plant, and mites take advantage of that.

This is also the time of year plants that spent the summer outdoors come back inside, sometimes carrying mites, aphids, or their eggs that a hard rinse and a few weeks outdoors would normally have kept in check. If you skipped a thorough pest check when you moved pots in — covered in more detail in [when to bring houseplants indoors](/blog/when-to-bring-houseplants-indoors/) — an outdoor infestation that was manageable in open air can explode once the plant is in a closed, dry room with no rain to knock mites off the foliage.

## Spider Mites or Something Else? How to Tell the Difference

Several common houseplant pests get lumped together because the symptoms — damaged leaves, a plant that looks "off" — overlap. Where you find the damage and what the pest itself looks like under a loupe will usually tell you which one you're dealing with.

| Pest | What you'll see | Where to look | What causes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spider mites | Fine stippling, bronzed leaves, silky webbing | Leaf undersides, stem joints, new growth | Warm, dry air |
| Fungus gnats | Small dark flies hovering near the plant | Soil surface, especially when watered | Soil that stays wet too long |
| Mealybugs | White, cottony clusters; sticky residue on leaves | Leaf axils, stem joints, under leaves | Warm, humid, crowded growth |
| Scale | Small brown or tan bumps that don't move | Stems and leaf midribs | Often arrives on new plants |
| Thrips | Slim, fast-moving dark insects; silvery scarring, black specks | Flower parts and new leaves | Warm weather, often from outdoors |

If what you're seeing is small flies around the pot rather than damage to the leaves themselves, you're more likely dealing with fungus gnats, which have a completely different cause and treatment — see [how to get rid of fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) for that process. Mealybugs and scale are both easier to spot by sight, since neither is as close to invisible as a mite, and neither spins webbing.

## How to Treat a Spider Mite Infestation, Step by Step

**What you'll need:**
- A sink, tub, or outdoor hose for rinsing
- Insecticidal soap or 0.5–1% neem oil solution (mixed per the product's label, typically around 1–2 teaspoons of neem oil and a few drops of mild dish soap as an emulsifier per quart of water)
- A spray bottle, 16–32 oz
- Pruning shears, wiped with rubbing alcohol between cuts
- A sealed plastic bag for disposing of trimmed material
- A space to isolate the plant, ideally a separate room

**1. Isolate the plant immediately.** Spider mites crawl from leaf to leaf and plant to plant where foliage touches, and they can also catch air currents and drift short distances. Move the affected plant at least several feet from your other plants — a separate room is better — before doing anything else.

**2. Prune out the worst damage.** Cut away leaves and stems that are heavily stippled, bronzed, or webbed. This removes a large share of the existing population in one step and reduces how much surface area you need to treat. Bag the trimmings and throw them out rather than composting them.

**3. Rinse the whole plant.** In a sink, tub, or with a hose outdoors if the weather allows, rinse every leaf — top and underside — with a steady stream of lukewarm water. This physically knocks mites and webbing off the plant and is genuinely effective on its own for light infestations, which is why it's worth doing even before you reach for a spray.

**4. Apply insecticidal soap or neem oil to every surface.** Spray until the solution drips off the leaves, paying particular attention to the undersides and the joints where leaves meet stems, since that's where mites concentrate. Do this in indirect light or in the evening, never in direct sun, since oil or soap on a leaf combined with strong sunlight can scorch the foliage.

**5. Repeat every five to seven days, for three full cycles.** This is the step people skip, and it's why mites seem to "come back" after treatment. A spray kills mites present on contact but doesn't reliably kill eggs, which hatch within three to ten days depending on temperature. Treating once interrupts the population; treating three times at five-to-seven-day intervals catches each new generation as it hatches, before it can lay more eggs.

**6. Keep the plant isolated and check it weekly.** Use the tap-and-paper test each week. Once you've gone two consecutive weeks with no mites found, it's reasonable to return the plant to its normal spot.

## Preventing Spider Mites From Coming Back

Since dry air is the main driver, the single most effective long-term prevention is raising humidity around susceptible plants once heating season starts — grouping plants together, using a humidifier, or setting pots on pebble trays all help, and the trade-offs between those methods are worth understanding before you pick one; see [raising humidity for houseplants once the heat kicks on](/blog/raise-humidity-for-houseplants-in-winter/) for a full comparison. Wiping down leaves periodically with a damp cloth also helps — it removes dust and any mites or eggs before they establish, and it's worth folding into whatever seasonal care routine you're already running; [adjusting your houseplant care for fall and winter](/blog/adjust-houseplant-care-for-fall-winter/) covers how watering, light, and feeding should shift at the same time.

New plants are a common entry point, too. Inspect anything you bring home — nursery stock, a gift plant, a cutting from a friend — before placing it near the rest of your collection, and keep it separate for a week or two if you have any doubt.

## Common Mistakes When Treating Spider Mites

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Spraying once and stopping | Eggs survive the spray and hatch days later | Repeat every 5–7 days for 3 full cycles |
| Spraying only the leaf tops | Mites live and feed on the undersides | Tilt leaves and spray from below, too |
| Using straight dish soap instead of insecticidal soap | Can strip the leaf's waxy coating and cause damage | Use a labeled insecticidal soap or properly diluted neem oil |
| Treating in bright, direct sun | Oil or soap plus strong sun can scorch leaves | Treat in indirect light or in the evening |
| Not isolating the plant | Mites spread to neighboring plants during treatment | Quarantine until two clean weekly checks in a row |
| Fixing the mites but not the dry air that caused them | Population rebounds in the same conditions | Pair treatment with a humidity fix for that spot |

## When to Cut Your Losses (or Ask for Help)

Most spider mite infestations respond to the rinse-and-treat routine above within three to four weeks. If a plant is still heavily infested after three full treatment cycles, or if the damage has spread to most of the foliage and new growth is also stippled, it may genuinely be more practical to let the plant go, especially if it's inexpensive or easily replaced — holding onto a severely damaged plant mainly risks reinfecting everything else nearby. For a valuable or hard-to-replace specimen, a local nursery or your state's cooperative extension office can help identify the specific mite species and recommend a stronger, correctly labeled miticide if soap and neem genuinely aren't working; this is less common than it sounds, since most indoor infestations are the two-spotted spider mite, which responds well to the standard approach.

Wear gloves when applying any treatment, keep pets and children away from a plant until the spray has fully dried, and always test a new product on one or two leaves first and wait 24 hours — some plants, particularly ferns and a few succulents, are more sensitive to oil-based treatments than others.

## FAQ

### Can spider mites spread to other houseplants?

Yes, easily. They crawl between touching leaves, can be carried on hands, tools, or clothing, and are light enough to drift short distances on air currents. This is why isolating an affected plant the moment you confirm an infestation matters more than almost any other step in treatment.

### Do spider mites bite humans or harm pets?

No. Spider mites feed exclusively on plant cells and have no interest in biting people or animals. The main risk to pets or kids is from the treatment products themselves if applied carelessly, not from the mites.

### Will cutting back a badly infested plant kill it?

Usually not, if the plant is otherwise healthy. Most houseplants tolerate losing a significant portion of damaged foliage and will push new growth once the infestation is under control, though a plant that's already stressed from low light or irregular watering will recover more slowly.

### How long does it take to get rid of spider mites completely?

Plan on three to four weeks for a full treatment cycle to run its course — three applications roughly a week apart, followed by two consecutive clean weekly checks before you consider the plant safe to move back with others. Stopping early is the most common reason mites appear to "come back."
