If you moved houseplants outside for the summer, don't wait for the first cold snap to bring them back in. By the time you feel the chill, they've already been stressed, and they may be carrying a few hitchhikers you don't want in your living room. A planned move over two or three weeks is far easier on the plants, and on you, than a rushed one the night a frost warning appears.

**Quick answer:** Start the process when nighttime lows begin dipping toward 55°F (13°C). Tropical houseplants should be inside before nights stay consistently in the low 50s. Spend the last week or two acclimating them to shade outdoors, and inspect and clean every plant before it crosses the threshold. Expect some leaf drop after the move. That's normal adjustment, not failure.

## Watch the Nighttime Temperature, Not the Calendar

The trigger is overnight lows, not a date. Early fall days can still reach the 70s while nights quietly slide into the 40s, so check the overnight forecast, not the daytime high. A single cool night rarely kills anything, but repeated nights below a plant's comfort zone show up as yellowing, dropped leaves or stalled growth within a couple of weeks.

Different plants have very different limits:

| Plant type | Examples | Bring inside when nights reach |
|---|---|---|
| Tender tropicals | Calathea, fiddle leaf fig, croton, most aroids | Around 55°F (13°C); earlier for the fussiest |
| Tough tropicals | Snake plant, ZZ plant, pothos, philodendron | The low 50s°F (about 10-12°C) |
| Succulents and cacti | Jade, aloe, echeveria | Around the mid-40s°F (about 7°C); keep them dry |
| Citrus and bay | Potted lemon, bay laurel | Before the first frost; they tolerate cool nights better than tropicals |
| Woody herbs | Rosemary, thyme | They handle light frost but should come in before a hard freeze |

If you're unsure about a plant, treat it as a tender tropical and bring it in early. Your local frost dates, which you can find by searching your area name plus "average first frost date", tell you roughly how much time you have left.

## A Week-by-Week Timeline for the Move

Rushing everything inside in one evening skips the steps that prevent pest outbreaks and leaf drop. This timeline spreads the work out:

| When | What to do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| About 3 weeks before the move | Stop fertilizing; start checking plants for pests | Plants shouldn't push out tender new growth just before a stressful change |
| About 2 weeks before | Move plants into shade outdoors (under a tree or a covered porch) | Starts adapting leaves to lower light before the bigger drop indoors |
| About 1 week before | Debug and clean each plant (see below); repot any that are root-bound | Easier and less messy to do outside |
| Move day | Bring plants in and place them by light needs | Brightest spots go to the sun-lovers |
| First 2-3 weeks inside | Water carefully, keep an eye out for pests, expect some leaf drop | Plants are recalibrating to less light and drier air |

## Inspect and Debug Every Plant Before It Comes Inside

This is the step people skip, and it matters most. Plants that spent the summer outside often bring pests in with them. Aphids, spider mites, mealybugs and scale are the usual culprits, and indoors they spread fast because nothing eats them.

![Close-up of houseplant leaves with pest damage spots, the kind of thing to check for before bringing plants indoors](../../assets/blog/bring-houseplants-indoors-carry.jpg)

Work through each plant like this:

1. **Check the undersides of leaves and where leaves meet stems.** Mites leave fine stippling and sometimes webbing. Mealybugs look like tiny tufts of cotton. Scale looks like small brown bumps that don't move.
2. **Look for sticky residue** on leaves or the surface below the pot. That's honeydew, a sign of aphids, scale or mealybugs.
3. **Rinse the foliage** with a gentle spray from the hose, including the undersides, to knock off anything you missed.
4. **Flush the soil.** Setting the pot in a bucket of water for about 15 minutes, with the water just below the rim, drives out ants, pill bugs and other soil dwellers. Let it drain fully afterward.
5. **Check the drainage holes** for slugs, earwigs or roots escaping into the saucer.
6. **Treat and isolate if you find pests.** Spray with insecticidal soap, following the label, and keep the plant away from your other plants for a week or two. Repeat the treatment as the label directs, since eggs often hatch after the first application.

Soil that stayed soggy outdoors can also bring in [fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/). Letting the top layer of soil dry out before and after the move is the simplest prevention.

## Clean Pots and Refresh the Soil Surface

While you're working on each plant, wipe down the pot and remove moss, algae and debris that built up over summer. Debris on a pot can harbor pests and fungal spores. If the top layer of soil looks compacted or crusted with mineral deposits, scrape off the top half-inch and replace it with fresh potting mix. That gives the plant a cleaner start without the disruption of a full [repot](/blog/how-to-repot-a-houseplant/). Plants often grow faster outdoors, so this is also a good moment to check whether roots are circling the pot or escaping from the drainage holes.

## Adjust for Lower Light and Drier Air

Even a bright indoor spot gets only a fraction of the light plants had outside, which is why the shade step in the timeline matters. Once inside, group plants by light needs rather than just available space. The plants that sat in full sun all summer need your brightest window, while shade-tolerant ones can sit further back.

Indoor air is also usually drier than outdoor air, especially once heating starts. Plants like calatheas and ferns may develop crispy leaf edges. Grouping plants together, setting pots on pebble trays or running a small humidifier helps; our guide on [raising humidity for houseplants](/blog/raise-humidity-for-houseplants-in-winter/) covers the options in detail.

## Watering and Feeding After the Move

Less light means slower growth and slower water use. Check the soil before every watering rather than keeping your summer schedule, which would overwater most plants indoors. Hold off on fertilizer until you see new growth, and for many plants wait until spring. Once they're settled, the routine in our guide to [adjusting houseplant care for fall and winter](/blog/adjust-houseplant-care-for-fall-winter/) takes over.

## Troubleshooting the First Weeks Indoors

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden drop of older leaves in the first two weeks | Normal adjustment to less light | Keep conditions stable; don't move the plant repeatedly |
| Yellow leaves with damp soil | Watering on the old summer schedule | Let soil dry more between waterings |
| Crispy brown leaf edges | Dry indoor air | Raise humidity; keep plants away from heating vents |
| Fine webbing or speckled leaves weeks later | Spider mites that survived inspection | Isolate, rinse, treat with insecticidal soap, repeat as labeled |
| Pale, stretched new growth | Not enough light | Move closer to a window or add a grow light |

## FAQ

### Can I bring plants inside and then put them back out on warm days?

You can, but frequent moves stress plants more than a single transition does. If you do it, keep them in the same light level each time. A shaded porch is better than full sun.

### Should I repot plants when I bring them in?

Only if they're clearly root-bound. Repotting adds stress on top of the light change. If a plant needs it, do it a week before the move, while it's still outside.

### Do I need to quarantine every plant?

It's good practice for any plant where you found pests or aren't sure. A week or two in a separate room lets any hidden eggs hatch where you can spot and treat them.

### Is some leaf drop after the move normal?

Yes. Many plants, especially ficus species, shed some older leaves while they adjust to lower light. As long as new growth looks healthy, the plant is adapting.
