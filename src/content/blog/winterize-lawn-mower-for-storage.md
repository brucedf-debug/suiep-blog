Once the grass stops growing — around the point your lawn is ready for its [last aeration and overseeding pass](/blog/how-to-aerate-and-overseed-your-lawn-in-fall/) of the year — a mower typically gets rolled into a corner of the garage and forgotten until spring. That gap between "last mow" and "first mow" is when most of a mower's avoidable problems get created. A gas engine left with old fuel in the tank, a battery stored at the wrong charge level, or a blade put away caked in grass clippings all cost nothing to prevent and a surprising amount to fix later. What a mower actually needs before winter depends on what powers it, and treating a cordless electric mower like a gas one is how people end up doing unnecessary work or skipping a step that actually mattered.

**Quick answer:** Gas mowers need the fuel either stabilized or fully drained, an oil change, a cleaned and inspected spark plug and air filter, and a sharpened blade before storage — skipping the fuel step is the single most common cause of no-start problems in spring. Battery-powered mowers store best with the battery removed, cleaned, and charged to roughly 40–60%, not left fully charged or fully dead, in a space that stays above freezing. Robotic mowers need their battery pulled and stored the same way, plus a check of the charging dock and boundary wire before the season's first run. Across all three types, a clean deck and a sharp, rust-free blade matter just as much as the fuel or battery step, and they're the part people skip most often.

## Match the Prep to Your Mower Type

Not every mower needs the same winterizing routine — the differences come down almost entirely to what's storing energy over the winter: gasoline, a lithium-ion battery, or nothing at all for a corded electric model.

| Mower type | Main winter risk | Effort level | What it needs most |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gas push mower** | Stale fuel gums up the carburetor | Moderate — 30–45 minutes | Fuel stabilizer or full drain, oil change, spark plug check |
| **Gas riding mower / tractor** | Same fuel issue, plus battery drain over winter | Higher — closer to an hour | Same fuel and oil steps, plus a trickle charger on the battery |
| **Corded electric mower** | Minimal — no fuel, no battery to manage | Low — 10 minutes | Clean deck, dry storage, cord inspection |
| **Cordless battery mower** | Battery degrades faster at full charge, full discharge, or in freezing temps | Low to moderate — 15 minutes | Remove battery, partial charge, store above freezing |
| **Robotic mower** | Same battery risk, plus a dock and boundary wire that sit outside all winter | Moderate — 20–30 minutes | Battery removal, dock weatherproofing, spring boundary check |

Gas mowers carry the most risk if skipped entirely, which is why the walkthrough below covers them first. Electric and robotic mowers are simpler, but "simpler" isn't "nothing to do" — a battery stored incorrectly for four or five months can lose a meaningful chunk of its lifespan.

## What You'll Need

- Fuel stabilizer (follow the bottle's ratio, typically about 1 oz per gallon) — only needed if you're not fully draining the tank
- Fresh engine oil rated for your mower (check the manual for weight; many small engines use SAE 30 or a 10W-30 equivalent)
- A funnel and a container rated for used oil disposal
- A socket wrench sized for your spark plug, plus a replacement if the old one is worn or fouled
- A stiff brush or putty knife for scraping caked grass off the deck's underside
- A file or sharpening tool, or a spare balanced blade to swap in
- Work gloves, cut-resistant ones for blade handling
- A trickle charger, for a riding mower's battery left in place over winter
- Light machine oil or silicone spray, for linkages and the deck's underside

## Step-by-Step: Winterizing a Gas Mower

1. **Run the tank low, then decide: stabilize or drain.** For storage under about three months, add fuel stabilizer and run the engine five minutes to circulate it through the carburetor. For longer storage, draining the tank fully with a siphon pump into an approved gas container avoids the problem entirely. Ethanol-blended gas left untreated can start separating and gumming up carburetor passages in as little as 30 days — the single biggest cause of mowers that won't start on the first pull in spring. The same logic applies to any small gas engine going into storage, including a gas [chainsaw](/blog/chainsaw-buying-guide/) between firewood seasons.
2. **Change the oil while the engine is still slightly warm from that last run,** so fresh oil goes into storage instead of oil that's held months of combustion byproducts against internal parts. Check your owner's manual for the correct weight and capacity.
3. **Pull the spark plug and check it.** Replace one with heavy carbon buildup, a worn electrode, or visible damage now rather than mid-season. With the plug out, it's also the safest moment to turn the blade, since the engine can't accidentally fire.
4. **Clean or replace the air filter.** A clogged filter restricts airflow and makes an engine run rich, adding to the same stale-fuel residue problem as skipping step 1. Paper filters usually need replacing; foam filters can be washed, dried and lightly re-oiled.
5. **Scrape the deck's underside clean.** Caked grass clippings hold moisture against the metal all winter, which is how a deck starts rusting from the inside out. A putty knife and stiff brush get most of it off; a hose finishes the job where water near electrical components isn't a concern (check your manual on any mower with onboard electronics).
6. **Remove, inspect, and sharpen the blade.** A dull or nicked blade tears grass instead of cutting it cleanly, and rust forms fastest on a blade left dirty over winter. Sharpen it yourself, or swap in a spare balanced blade and send the old one out for sharpening before next season.
7. **Lubricate exposed linkages and cables** with light machine oil, and wipe bare metal with a light coat of oil to prevent surface rust.
8. **On riding mowers and tractors, address the battery separately.** Disconnect it and bring it indoors, or leave it on a trickle charger (a battery maintainer) so it doesn't fully discharge over months of sitting unused — a battery left connected with no maintainer, and no disconnect either, is a common reason riding mowers won't start in spring.
9. **Store it covered and off bare concrete if possible,** in a dry space — concrete conducts cold and moisture into metal parts more than a wood pallet or rubber mat underneath.

## Winterizing Cordless Battery and Corded Electric Mowers

Electric mowers skip the fuel problem entirely, which makes their routine shorter, but it's not nothing.

**Corded electric mowers** need almost no special treatment: clean the deck the same way as a gas mower (the same scraping routine applies whether or not you mulch-mowed fall leaves with it, the way our guide to [raking leaves efficiently](/blog/how-to-rake-leaves-efficiently/) covers), check the cord for nicks or exposed wire — a damaged cord is a shock hazard, not just a maintenance issue — sharpen or replace the blade, and store it somewhere dry. There's no fuel or battery chemistry to manage, so this is the lowest-effort mower type to put away for the season.

**Cordless battery mowers** need more thought because of how lithium-ion batteries age. Storing one fully charged or fully drained for months accelerates capacity loss more than storing it mid-range. The practical routine:

- Remove the battery from the mower rather than leaving it connected.
- Charge or discharge it to roughly 40–60% before storage — check your specific manufacturer's guidance, since the ideal range varies slightly by brand.
- Store it indoors, somewhere that stays above freezing and ideally below about 80°F; a garage that regularly drops well below freezing is harder on the battery than a closet or basement.
- Check it once or twice over the winter, and top off the charge if it's dropped significantly, rather than leaving it completely untouched for four or five months.

If you're also storing a cordless [leaf blower](/blog/leaf-blower-buying-guide/) for the season, the same battery logic applies regardless of which tool it's powering.

## Robotic Mowers: What's Different

A robotic mower adds two things a push mower doesn't have to think about: a charging dock that usually stays outside all winter, and a boundary wire pinned or buried around the lawn's edge.

- **Remove the battery** the same way as a cordless push mower — don't leave it docked and trickle-charging all winter unless your manufacturer explicitly recommends that for your model.
- **Bring the mower itself indoors** rather than leaving it in an unheated dock, even if the dock is rated for outdoor use year-round.
- **Decide whether the dock and boundary wire stay in place or come up**, based on your snow load and whether plows or snow removal equipment could catch and pull the wire.
- **In spring, inspect the boundary wire for breaks** before the first run — a mower that worked fine last fall but won't finish a mapping run has usually found a wire nicked by a rake, an edger, or frost heave.

## Common Winterizing Mistakes

| Mistake | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Mower won't start in spring | Old fuel left in the tank all winter without stabilizer | Stabilize or fully drain fuel every time, not just "most years" |
| Rust on the deck underside | Grass clippings left caked on before storage | Scrape and clean the deck every time you winterize, not just when it looks bad |
| Battery holds much less charge by spring | Stored fully charged, fully dead, or in a freezing space | Store at a partial charge, indoors, above freezing |
| Riding mower battery dead in spring | Left connected with no trickle charger over months of inactivity | Disconnect it or use a battery maintainer, not both skipped |
| Blade tears grass instead of cutting | Dull or nicked blade put away without sharpening | Sharpen or swap the blade as part of the winterizing routine, not as a separate spring task |
| Robotic mower won't map the yard in spring | Boundary wire nicked over winter by other yard tools or snow removal | Inspect the full wire run before the first spring cycle |

## Safety Notes and When to Call a Professional

- Always disconnect the spark plug wire before working near the blade, even with the engine off — the single most important habit for avoiding an accidental start while your hands are near the deck.
- Dispose of old gasoline and used oil at a hazardous waste or auto parts drop-off location; neither belongs down a drain or in household trash.
- Never store a lithium-ion battery in direct contact with flammable materials, and take one that's swollen, cracked, or unusually hot to a battery recycling center rather than storing it at all.
- If a carburetor is already gummed up from a past season of untreated fuel, a small-engine repair shop can usually clean or rebuild it for less than a new engine costs.
- A riding mower or tractor with a drive belt, deck spindle, or steering issue is worth a technician's look rather than a DIY attempt — more moving parts under tension than a push mower.

## FAQ

### Do I really need to drain the gas tank, or is stabilizer enough?

For storage under about three months, stabilizer added to a full tank with the engine run briefly to circulate it is generally enough. For longer storage, or if you're not confident you'll start the mower again before spring, fully draining the tank removes the risk entirely.

### Can I store my mower outside under just a tarp?

A tarp blocks rain and snow but doesn't address the cold or moisture that collects underneath it, especially on bare concrete. A dry garage or shed is better whenever one is available; if outdoor storage is your only choice, use a fitted mower cover and put something underneath the mower to break contact with concrete or damp ground.

### How do I know if gas in the tank has gone bad?

Old, untreated gas often develops a sour or stale smell different from fresh fuel, and it may look darker or slightly cloudy than new gasoline. If you're unsure, it's simpler and cheaper to drain it and start fresh than to run questionable fuel through the engine and risk a clogged carburetor.

### Is it bad to store a lawn mower battery fully charged over winter?

Yes, for lithium-ion batteries specifically. Keeping a battery at 100% charge for months accelerates capacity loss compared to the 40–60% range. The same is true at the other extreme — a fully drained battery also loses usable life, which is why checking the charge once or twice over the winter is worth the few minutes it takes.
